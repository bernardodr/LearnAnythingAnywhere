Lesson 1 · System Design Interviews

A map for the 45 minutes

Before you learn a single component, learn the route through the interview.

Your win today You'll be able to name the six steps of a system design interview in order, say roughly how long each takes, and write the requirements for a real prompt (Bitly). That first step is the one mid-level candidates are expected to lead.

Why a framework comes first

You might expect the hard part of a design round to be knowing about caches and databases. For most candidates the more common failure is running out of time without a working design, or drifting into one corner of the problem. A fixed sequence of steps with time budgets keeps you focused and makes sure you deliver a complete system.[1]

At mid-level, interviewers expect you to guide the early parts yourself: requirements, APIs, schemas and the high-level design. In the later stages they'll often steer you toward the interesting problems.[2] So the framework is the part of the interview you control.

Step 1 up close: requirements

Requirements come in two kinds:

  • Functional requirements are what the system does, phrased as "Users should be able to…". Ask clarifying questions, then narrow to roughly the top three.
  • Non-functional requirements are the qualities the system must have: scalability, latency, availability vs. consistency, durability, security. Pick the 3–5 that matter most and put numbers on them where you can ("redirects in <100 ms", not "fast").

Two traps to avoid:[1]

  1. The kitchen-sink list. A long feature list hurts more than it helps. Every feature you list is one you have to design.
  2. Math for its own sake. Only do capacity estimates when the result would change your design. Otherwise say you'll do the math as it becomes relevant.
Watch out for this habit If you're used to feature work, you may want to jump straight to drawing services. Resist it. In the interview, the requirements you write down become the yardstick the interviewer uses to judge your design.
